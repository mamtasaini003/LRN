\begin{abstract}
Neural operators excel at approximating PDE solution maps but often suffer from spectral aliasing, hallucinated modes, and poor long-term rollout stability due to unconstrained latent representations. We propose the Latent Reciprocity Representations (LRR), which uses a bidirectional contrastive alignment between latent embeddings of backbone features and solutions, enforcing reciprocity via an InfoNCE loss to constrain operators within physically admissible manifolds. The work is introduced as a backbone-agnostic framework for augmenting neural operators with a bidirectional latent space that integrates seamlessly with architectures. The key principle is to enforce reciprocity between latent embeddings of target fields and operator outputs, thereby constraining the learned operator to evolve within a data-driven manifold of the solution. We have trained LRR for Burgers', 2D Darcy, and 2D Navier-Stokes problems. The framework has shown improvement, confirming the latent embedding alignment training to be worth it. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Neural Operators \cite{azizzadenesheli2024neural,JMLR:v24:21-1524} are a novel class of neural networks designed to learn infinite-dimensional functionals. Their most relevant application is learning surrogate maps for the solution operators of partial differential equations (PDEs). Solving PDEs efficiently and accurately using numerical methods remains an open problem. In fact, designing an ideal PDE solver requires satisfying a long list of often competing requirements, such as accuracy, generalization across different PDEs, the ability to operate on high-resolution spatiotemporal grids, robustness to varying domain topologies and geometries, boundary conditions, dimensionality, and solution-space smoothness. Deep learning offers a general-purpose, plug-and-play framework for modeling a wide class of problems, avoiding problem-dependent design choices.

The latent representations learned by neural operators are shaped during training through gradient-based updates that minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between model predictions and ground-truth outputs. As demonstrated in \cite{zhou2024masked}, employing Masked Autoencoders \cite{he2022masked} loss for pre-training in the context of partial differential equations (PDEs) helps structure latent feature maps, leading to improved performance and enhanced transferability to previously unseen equations. More recently, advances in computer vision have shown that explicitly signaling model weights by imposing a representation-level loss directly in the latent space can significantly improve the quality of learned features \cite{oord2018representation, caron2021emerging, grill2020bootstrap}.

Building on this strength, we introduce Latent Reciprocity Representation (LRR), a training paradigm that enforces explicit latent-space regularization by aligning input and output representations via the InfoNCE (Information Noise Contrastive Estimation) loss \cite{oord2018representation}. Our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a novel training paradigm for Latent Reciprocity Representation in Neural Operators based on the combination of the InfoNCE and MSE losses.
    \item We run a set of preliminary experiments to validate our claims on three different PDEs: Darcy Flow, Burgers' equation, and Navier-Stokes equation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Related Work} Neural operators \cite{azizzadenesheli2024neural, hao2023gnot}, such as FNO \cite{lifourier,tran2023factorized,li2023fourier,li2024physics}, Graph Neural Networks \cite{brandstettermessage, sanchez2020learning}, Equivariant Graph Neural Networks \cite{toshev20233}, or combinations of GNNs and FNOs \cite{li2023geometryinformed}, are popular approaches for simulating physical systems. Other notable models include convolution-based architectures with channel attention \cite{takamoto2023learning} and U-Net–based models \cite{williams2023a, zhang2024sinenet}. PINNs \cite{raissi2019physics} are another important class of methods, although they are often harder to train \cite{krishnapriyan2021characterizing, deoperator}. Additional relevant work includes \cite{serranozebra}, which trains a tokenizer for solving in-context learning problems, as well as approaches that combine machine learning with classical solvers \cite{um2020solver, thuerey2021physics, bar2019unsupervised}.

\section{Background}

We aim to learn the mapping
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{G} \colon & \Omega \to \Omega' \\
    & f \mapsto u
\end{align*}
where $\Omega, \Omega'$ are function spaces, e.g.\ $\Omega = L^2(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_f})$ and $\Omega' = L^2(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_u})$ for a spatial domain $D \subset \mathbb{R}^d$.
The function $f$ denotes input fields (e.g.\ forcing terms, initial conditions, or coefficients) and $u$ the corresponding PDE solution (e.g.\ Poisson, Darcy, or Navier–Stokes).

We approximate $\mathcal{G}$ with a neural operator $\mathcal{G}_\theta$ trained on data
$\{(f_i^n, u_i^n)\}_{i=1}^l$ at resolution $n$ by minimizing the mean squared error
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}
= \frac{1}{l} \sum_{i=1}^l \left\| \mathcal{G}_\theta(f_i^n) - u_i^n \right\|_2^2 .
\] 

In the time-dependent case, $f$ can be interpreted as the initial condition and $u$ as the solution at a specific time step, as in the heat equation $u_t = \Delta u$, where, for example, $f = u_0$ and $u = u_1$.

We use a 2D Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) as a backbone. Let $v_t(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{c_t}$ denote the feature field at layer $t$, and let $\hat{v}_t(k) = \mathcal{F}(v_t)(k)$ be its Fourier transform. A single Fourier layer updates
\[
v_{t+1}(x)
=
\sigma\!\left(
W_t v_t(x)
+
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(
\sum_{|k|\le K}
R^{(t)}_k \,\hat{v}_t(k) \, e^{2\pi i\,k\cdot x}
\right)
\right),
\qquad
R^{(t)}_k \in \mathbb{C}^{c_{t+1}\times c_t},
\]
where $W_t$ is a learned pointwise linear map, $\sigma$ is a nonlinearity, and $R^{(t)}_k = 0$ for $|k| > K$.
Stacking $K_{\text{layers}}$ such layers yields
\[
v_K = T_\theta(\ell_0(f)),
\]
where $\ell_0$ lifts $f$ to the feature space and $T_\theta$ denotes the FNO backbone with parameters $\theta$.
The final prediction is obtained via a decoder $\Pi$ as
\[
\hat{u} = \Pi(v_K).
\]


\section{Method}

\paragraph{Latent encoders.}
We introduce two encoders:
\begin{align*}
    z_v &= E_v(v_K;\phi_v) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}, \\
    z_u &= E_u(u;\phi_u) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z},
\end{align*}
where $E_v$ maps the backbone feature field $v_K$ to a global latent vector, and $E_u$ maps the ground-truth solution $u$ to a latent vector of the same dimension $d_z$ (e.g.\ via a small CNN + global pooling).

Given a minibatch $\{(f_i, u_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, we compute
\[
v_{K,i} = T_\theta(\ell_0(f_i)), \quad
z_{v,i} = E_v(v_{K,i}), \quad
z_{u,i} = E_u(u_i).
\]

\paragraph{Latent reciprocity via InfoNCE.}
We define the contrastive loss between $z_v$ and $z_u$ as
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}
=
-\sum_{i=1}^N
\log
\frac{
\exp\big( \mathrm{sim}(z_{v,i}, z_{u,i})/\tau \big)
}{
\sum_{j=1}^N \exp\big( \mathrm{sim}(z_{v,i}, z_{u,j})/\tau \big)
},
\]
where $\mathrm{sim}$ is cosine similarity and $\tau>0$ is the temperature.
Here $(z_{v,i}, z_{u,i})$ form positive pairs, while $(z_{v,i}, z_{u,j})$ for $j\neq i$ act as negatives within the batch.

\paragraph{Total loss.}
The training objective combines data fidelity and latent reciprocity:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\hat{u}, u)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{nce}} \, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}(z_v, z_u),
\]
with $\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{nce}} > 0$ controlling the relative weight of reconstruction and latent alignment.

\paragraph{Two-stage protocol.}
We use a two-stage training scheme:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Hybrid optimisation).} Train $\theta, \phi_f, \phi_u, \phi_v$ jointly on $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}$.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Distillation).} Discard $E_u$ and $E_v$; fine-tune $\theta$ (and optionally $E_f$) using only $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}$ to obtain the final deployable operator $f \mapsto \hat{u}$.
\end{itemize}
At inference time, only the path $f \xrightarrow{\mathcal{G}_\theta} \hat{u}$ remains active; the projection heads $E_v$ and $E_u$ and contrastive terms are evaluated only during training.


\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Training and Data Details}

We trained all the models on NVIDIA A100 GPU, enforcing reproducibility via a fixed random seed of 42. Datasets were sourced from \cite{li2023fourier}, including 1D Burgers' equation on 128 spatial points ($\nu=0.1$, mapping initial condition to $t=1$ solution); 2D Darcy flow at 64x64 resolution (subsampled from 85x85 permeability fields); and 2D steady Navier-Stokes vorticity at 128x128 (subsampled at 64x64 for training, forcing to fixed-time output). Data were normalised per dataset, with inputs uniformly lifted to 32 channels in the FNO backbone with hyperparameters mentioned in Table \ref{tab:training-hyperparams}.

Training employed joint optimisation of
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{mse}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\hat{u}, u)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{nce}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}(z_v, z_u),
\]
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}$ is the field reconstruction loss and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}$ is the bidirectional latent contrastive loss defined above.
We set $\tau = 0.07$ and $d_z = 64$ in all experiments.


We use a 2-Stage training protocol: \textbf{Stage 1 Hybrid Optimisation} minimises the joint loss of data and the latent space. \textbf{Stage 2 Distillation} trains only on $\mathcal{L}_{mse}$ to refine the forward deployable model.  We used Adam (weight decay of $1e^{-4}$), monitoring convergence via validation relative $L_2$ error plateau ($<1e^{-4}$ drop over 10 epochs).

\subsection{Latent Visualization}

The LRR framework explicitly targets alignment of the predicted solution in a dedicated latent space. After the Fourier blocks produce latent features, these representations retain substantially richer structural information than what is exploited by a standard MSE objective on the output field alone. The core idea is therefore to guide learning directly in this latent manifold: rather than treating the latent codes as a passive by-product of the forward pass, LRR performs a dedicated latent optimisation that shapes the backbone features throughout training, instead of only correcting errors at the final layer.

To quantify this effect, we compare the latent representations of FNO and LRR-FNO against those obtained from an encoder applied to the ground-truth solutions. This analysis shows that the LRR framework enforces a much tighter alignment between backbone features and the ground-truth latent codes, achieving cosine similarity scores exceeding 0.9, whereas the vanilla FNO exhibits markedly weaker correspondence.

\section{Conclusion}

Our LRR framework shows consistent gains over the backbone model across Burgers', Darcy and Navier-Stokes benchmarks. Latent InfoNCE alignment maps to the target values without adding to the computation, especially for non-linear problems. \textbf{Future work} includes the implementation of the LRR framework with physics-informed losses to reduce the data needs for industrial deployment. Overall, the LRR framework is useful for any operator model where the features are to be semantically tied to targets rather than just pixel-matching.

\section{Additional Analysis}

\subsection{Structural Advantages}

\textbf{Eliminating Hallucinations.} Adding a latent constraint using latent representations allows the field to align structurally, avoiding hallucinations. 

\textbf{Spectral Aliasing Reduction.} The latent vector $z_v$ adds a modulation to the spectra of Fourier layers in such a way that it effectively removes unnecessary high-frequency noise.

\textbf{Implicit Regularizer.} Learning the right mapping helps the model to recognize the way forward to the target field. It is also necessary that the model learns what not to focus on while mapping to the required function, which makes it a powerful regularizer. Alignment of the latent structure helps the model to focus on physical modes that capture the difference between multiple simulations.

\subsection{Concept of Reciprocity}

\textbf{RECIPROCITY} here refers to a bidirectional consistent framework that acts as a regularizer and modular, exploiting more information that the latent space that is the "hidden space" contains.

Standard neural operators learn one-way mapping. They don't have a way to verify that the internal representations are physically valid in a supervised setting. In the LRN framework, the model ensures the latent features of the input are constrained by the known properties of the target solutions. While transforming to the frequency domain, the model might tend to hallucinate unphysical modes. These modes lack similarity to the known solution manifold in the latent space, resulting in a high contrastive penalty.


\section{Extended Experimental Results}

We assess LRR-FNO across five complementary experiments that probe its predictive accuracy, internal representations, resolution invariance, noise resilience, and shape generalization-each building on the GAOT benchmark from Section 3. Performance against a vanilla FNO baseline reveals how latent reciprocity systematically sharpens solution fidelity and robustness.

We evaluate LRR-FNO on point-cloud datasets from the GAOT benchmarks, capturing steady-state elliptic PDE solutions across six 2D geometries: circle (\texttt{Circle.nc}), cone (\texttt{Cone-F.nc}), three ellipses with ratios 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 (\texttt{Ellipse-*.nc}), and semicircle (\texttt{Semicircle-F.nc}). Each sample encodes the operator $\mathcal{G}: c(x,y) \mapsto u(x,y)$ solving
\[
- \nabla \cdot \bigl(a(x)\,\nabla u(x)\bigr) = f(x) \quad \text{in } \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2,
\]
where $c$ combines constant diffusion $a\equiv 0.3$ (channel 0), domain mask $m\in[0.5, 2.5]$ (channel 1) and forcing $f\in[0,10.43]$ (channel 2), with coordinates $x,y\in[-9,9]$. Raw data has unstructured clouds $[B, 1, 14000_\text{pts}, N_\text{feat}]$ (Table~\ref{tab:gaot-datasets}).

The data preprocessing is interpolated via \texttt{scipy.griddata} (linear) to $64\times64$ grids, adding 10\% padding, and standard normalization channels, yielding $c\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W}$, $u\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times H\times W}$ for FNO lifting. This preserves mesh invariance while standardising inputs.

\subsection{OOD Generalization (Exp.~5)}
Circle-trained models face unseen elliptic/cone/semicircle geometries: LRR-FNO averages 20.5\% $L_2$ gain (peaks 39\% on extreme AR2.5), where FNO collapses to geometry-specific artifacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:ood_gen}). Latent supervision during training breaks shape overfitting, yielding a universal PDE operator that transfers across domains.

Across regimes, LRR-FNO's edge stems from mid-network feature correction—FNO matches pixels, but reciprocity matches physics.
